## Key Takeaway
Surveys are powerful for capturing broad user sentiment, but their validity depends entirely on mitigating systematic biases in question design and sampling.

## 1. Survey Design Fundamentals
Surveys are used to collect **quantitative data** about user attitudes, behaviors, and demographics.

### Choosing the Right Scales
-   **Likert Scales**: Used to measure agreement (e.g., Strongly Disagree to Strongly Agree).
-   **Semantic Differential**: Measures polar opposites (e.g., Boring to Exciting).

## 2. Mitigating Survey Biases
In an exam context, identifying these biases is a "Hard Red Line".
-   **Satisficing**: Users provide "good enough" answers to finish quickly (e.g., choosing the middle option for everything).
-   **Acquiescence Bias**: The tendency to agree with all statements.
-   **Social Desirability Bias**: Users answer in a way they think the researcher wants to hear or what is socially "correct".
-   **Response Order Bias**: Users are more likely to pick the first few options in a long list (Primacy effect).
<Callout type="warn">
**Mitigation Strategy**: Use a mix of positively and negatively phrased questions to detect "straight-lining" or acquiescence.
</Callout>

## 3. Mixed-Methods Research
Mixed methods combine **Qualitative** (the 'why') and **Quantitative** (the 'how much') data to provide a holistic view.

### The System Usability Scale (SUS)
SUS is a "quick and dirty" way to measure usability.
-   10 questions with Likert scales.
-   **Nuance**: A SUS score is a number from 0-100, but it is **not a percentage**. A score of 68 is considered average.

## 4. Examiner's Perspective

### Hard Red Lines: Bias Identification
If an exam scenario shows a survey with only positive questions, you **must** identify "Acquiescence Bias" as a risk.

### Technical Nuance: Sampling
-   **Random Sampling**: Every member of the population has an equal chance.
-   **Convenience Sampling**: Choosing whoever is available. **Warning**: This leads to low external validity (results can't be generalized).

### Failure Analysis: Double-Barreled Questions
Avoid questions like "Is this app fast and easy to use?". If a user says "No", you don't know if it was slow or hard to use.
