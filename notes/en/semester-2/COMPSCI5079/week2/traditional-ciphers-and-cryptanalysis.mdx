## Key Takeaway

Traditional ciphers, while foundational to cryptography, primarily relied on obfuscation techniques like **transposition** (rearranging plaintext units) and **substitution** (replacing plaintext units). Their security often hinged on key length and complexity. However, most traditional ciphers are vulnerable to statistical attacks, particularly **frequency analysis**, due to the inherent redundancy of natural languages. Concepts like **confusion** (obscuring the relationship between ciphertext and key) and **diffusion** (spreading the influence of plaintext statistics over ciphertext) emerged as critical principles for stronger encryption, laying the groundwork for modern cryptosystems. The evolution from simple ciphers to complex mechanical devices like the Enigma machine highlights the continuous arms race between cryptographers and cryptanalysts.

## Traditional Encryption Schemes

Traditional encryption schemes predate electronic computers and are primarily single-key systems. They are broadly categorized into two main types:
-   **Transposition ciphers**: Characters are rearranged without changing their identity.
-   **Substitution ciphers**: Characters are replaced by other characters, symbols, or numbers.

Both methods can be combined, and their effectiveness is often evaluated against various types of attacks.

### Types of Attack

Cryptanalysis attempts to break codes using available information:
-   **Ciphertext Only**: Attacker only has the ciphertext.
-   **Known Plaintext**: Attacker has a segment of plaintext corresponding to a segment of ciphertext.
-   **Chosen Plaintext**: Attacker can choose a plaintext and obtain its corresponding ciphertext.

Common attack methods include:
-   **Brute Force (Exhaustive Search)**: Trying all possible keys.
-   **Frequency Analysis**: Exploiting the statistical properties of language (e.g., letter, digram, trigram frequencies).

<Callout type="info" title="Confusion and Diffusion">
**Confusion** aims to obscure the relationship between the ciphertext and the key, meaning a small change in the key should produce a large, unpredictable change in the ciphertext.
**Diffusion** aims to spread the influence of a single plaintext bit over many ciphertext bits, meaning a small change in the plaintext should result in a large, unpredictable change in the ciphertext. Both are crucial for robust cryptographic design.
</Callout>

### Transposition Ciphers

Transposition ciphers rearrange the order of plaintext characters within fixed-length blocks based on a key-dependent permutation. Decryption uses the inverse permutation.

-   **Example**: Text "securityandcryptography" arranged in 4-character blocks and permuted.
-   **Unicity Distance**: Relates to the key space entropy (e.g., `log2(d!)` for block length `d`).
-   **Cryptanalysis**: Transposition ciphers preserve letter frequencies, making them susceptible to frequency analysis combined with anagramming techniques, especially with di-gram and tri-gram frequency tables.

### Substitution Ciphers

Substitution ciphers replace plaintext letters with other letters or symbols.
-   **Shift Substitution**: Each letter is shifted a fixed amount along the alphabet (e.g., Caesar Cipher, where `c = (p + k) % n`).
    -   **Weakness**: Poor diffusion (changing one plaintext letter changes only one ciphertext letter) and susceptible to frequency analysis (shifted frequencies).
-   **Multiplicative Substitution**: Uses `c = (p * k) % n`, with decryption using the multiplicative inverse.
-   **Combined Substitution**: `c = (p * k1 + k2) % n`.
-   **Cryptanalysis**: Small unicity distance and vulnerable to single-letter frequency analysis and brute force, as the key space is relatively small.

### Homophonic Ciphers

Homophonic ciphers map each plaintext letter to potentially *more than one* ciphertext symbol.
-   **Goal**: To flatten the frequency distribution of ciphertext symbols, thereby weakening single-letter frequency analysis. More frequent plaintext letters are assigned more ciphertext options.
-   **Weakness**: Can still be vulnerable to di-gram frequencies.
-   **Second-Order Homophonic Ciphers**: An advanced technique to hide two different plaintext messages with different keys within a single ciphertext, often used for deniability (e.g., "rubber hose" decryption).

### Polyalphabetic Ciphers

Polyalphabet ciphers use multiple different substitutions for different plaintext letters, often cycling through a set of substitution rules.
-   **Vigenère/Beaufort Cipher**: A well-known example where the shift amount for each plaintext letter depends on a repeating key. Formula: `ci = (pi + ki) % n`.
-   **Cryptanalysis (Kasiski's Method)**: Exploits the repeating nature of the key by looking for repeated ciphertext segments that correspond to identical plaintext phrases, whose distances are multiples of the key period.

### Rotor Machines

Mechanical encryption devices that implement polyalphabetic substitutions.
-   **Mechanism**: Multiple rotors, each performing a substitution. Rotors rotate after each character encryption, changing the substitution alphabet.
-   **Advantages**: Offer excellent confusion and diffusion, making them robust against Kasiski's attack due to very long periods.

### The Enigma Machine

A sophisticated rotor machine used by Germany in WWII.
-   **Components**: Multiple rotors, a reflector (a fixed rotor that bounces the current back through the rotors), and a plugboard (a simple permutation device).
-   **Key Management**: The key involved rotor choice, order, and plugboard settings, changed daily.
-   **Indicator Keys**: A crucial operational procedure where a message-specific key (indicator key) was encrypted using daily settings, then used to configure the machine for the actual message. This prevented identical plaintext messages from yielding identical ciphertext.
-   **Breaking the Enigma**: Accomplished mainly through **known plaintext attacks** (using "cribs" or probable words like "ANX", "EINS") and **brute force** using mechanical "Bombes".
-   **Weaknesses**: The reflector was a critical weakness, as it ensured a letter could never be encrypted to itself, which significantly reduced the key space that cryptanalysts needed to search.

### Running Key Ciphers

Ciphers where the key is the same length as the plaintext, often derived from a long source.
-   **Encryption/Decryption**: Simple shift substitution `ci = (pi + ki) mod n`, `pi = (ci - ki) mod n`.
-   **Literature-based Keys**: Using a book or document as the key source. This appears to defeat frequency analysis and periodicity but is vulnerable to **Friedman's Attack**.
-   **Friedman's Attack**: Exploits the redundancy of natural language in *both* plaintext and a literature-based key to find high-frequency letter combinations that could produce observed ciphertext, greatly reducing possible key/plaintext pairs.
-   **Vernam Cipher / One-Time Pad (OTP)**: A special case of a running key cipher using **XOR** (`ci = pi XOR ki`). If the key is truly random, never reused, and at least as long as the plaintext, it provides **perfect secrecy** and is unbreakable.
    -   **Practical Limitation**: The key distribution problem due to the key's length.
-   **Pseudo-Random Number Generators (PRNGs) for Running Keys**: Using a PRNG to generate a long keystream from a short seed.
    -   **Vulnerability**: Generic PRNGs introduce exploitable structure. Only cryptographically secure PRNGs should be used.
    -   **Lorenz Cipher**: A Vernam cipher used by Germany in WWII, whose PRNG weakness was exploited by differential cryptanalysis, leading to its break by Colossus.

### Transition to Block Ciphers

The limitations of traditional ciphers, especially against frequency attacks, highlighted the need for more complex designs. Encrypting blocks of characters (polygram substitution) rather than single letters was a conceptual step towards modern **block ciphers**, enabled by electronic computers.
