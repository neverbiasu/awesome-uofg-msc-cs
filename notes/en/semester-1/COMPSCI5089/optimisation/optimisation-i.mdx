## Overview
- **Goal**: To understand how optimisation is used to "learn" the parameters of a model by minimizing an objective function.
- **Scope**: This note covers objective functions, constraints, convexity, and an in-depth look at Gradient Descent, Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD), and Mini-Batch Gradient Descent.

## Core Concepts of Optimisation

Optimisation is the process of finding the best set of parameters (or weights) for a model that minimizes or maximizes a certain function, known as the objective function.

### Objective Function
- The **objective function**, often denoted as $$J(\theta)$$, measures how well the model is performing. In machine learning, this is typically a **cost** or **loss** function that quantifies the error between the model's predictions and the actual data.
- Our goal is to find the parameters $$\theta$$ that result in the lowest possible value of $$J(\theta)$$.

### Constraints
- **Constraints** are conditions that the parameters must satisfy. For example, a parameter might be constrained to be non-negative.
- **Unconstrained optimisation** problems have no such conditions.

## Convex vs. Non-Convex Optimisation

<Callout title="The Shape of the Problem">
The geometry of the objective function's surface determines the difficulty of the optimisation problem.
</Callout>

### Convex Optimisation
- An optimisation problem is **convex** if the objective function has a bowl-like shape.
- **Key Property**: A convex function has only one minimum, which is the **global minimum**.
- This makes the problem relatively easy to solve, as any local minimum found is guaranteed to be the best possible solution.

### Non-Convex Optimisation
- A **non-convex** function has a complex, irregular surface with multiple "valleys" and "hills".
- **Key Challenge**: These functions have numerous **local minima** (the bottom of a valley) in addition to the **global minimum** (the lowest valley of all).
- Most deep learning problems are highly non-convex.

## Gradient Descent

Gradient Descent is an iterative optimisation algorithm used to find a local minimum of a differentiable function.

- **Intuition**: Imagine you are standing on a hillside and want to get to the bottom. You would look around to see which direction goes downhill most steeply and take a step in that direction. You repeat this process until you can't go any further down.
- The "steepest direction" is the **negative of the gradient** of the objective function, $$- \nabla J(\theta)$$.

### The Update Rule
The core of Gradient Descent is the update rule, applied iteratively:

$$\theta_{\text{new}} = \theta_{\text{old}} - \alpha \nabla J(\theta_{\text{old}})$$

- $$\theta$$: The parameters of the model.
- $$\alpha$$: The **learning rate**, a small scalar that controls the size of each step.
- $$\nabla J(\theta)$$: The gradient of the objective function with respect to the parameters.

<Callout title="The Learning Rate α" type="warn">
Choosing the right learning rate is critical:
- **Too small**: The algorithm will converge very slowly.
- **Too large**: The algorithm may overshoot the minimum and fail to converge, potentially even diverging.
</Callout>

## Variants of Gradient Descent

The main difference between the variants of Gradient Descent is how much data is used to compute the gradient at each step.

### Batch Gradient Descent
- The gradient is computed using the **entire training dataset** in every iteration.
- **Pros**: Guarantees convergence to the global minimum for convex problems and a local minimum for non-convex problems.
- **Cons**: Very slow and computationally expensive for large datasets.

### Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD)
- The gradient is computed using a **single, randomly selected training example** in every iteration.
- **Pros**: Much faster and can escape shallow local minima due to its noisy updates.
- **Cons**: The path to the minimum is very erratic (stochastic). It may not converge to the exact minimum but will oscillate around it.

### Mini-Batch Gradient Descent
- A compromise between the two extremes. The gradient is computed using a **small, random subset (a "mini-batch")** of the training data.
- **Pros**: Offers a good balance between the stability of Batch GD and the speed of SGD. It's the most common variant used in deep learning.
- **Cons**: Introduces an additional hyperparameter (the batch size).
