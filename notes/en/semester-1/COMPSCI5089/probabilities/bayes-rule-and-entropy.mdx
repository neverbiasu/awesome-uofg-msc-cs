## Overview
- **Objective**: To understand and apply Bayes' Rule to solve problems involving conditional probabilities, and to calculate and interpret the entropy of a probability distribution.
- **Scope**: This note covers the concepts of joint, marginal, and conditional probabilities, Bayes' rule with its applications, and the concept of entropy. It uses text analysis and a plague diagnosis problem as examples.

## Learning Objectives
1. Understand the definitions of joint, marginal, and conditional probability.
2. Be able to apply Bayes' Rule to invert conditional probabilities.
3. Understand the concept of entropy and its relationship to information and uncertainty.
4. Be able to calculate the entropy of a discrete probability distribution.

## Key Knowledge Points

### Joint, Marginal, and Conditional Probabilities
- **Motivation**: To model the relationships between multiple random variables, we need more than just the probability of a single event. We need to describe the probabilities of combinations of events.
- **Core Concepts**:
    - **Joint Probability `P(X,Y)`**: The probability of two events X and Y happening simultaneously.
    - **Marginal Probability `P(X)`**: The probability of a single event X, irrespective of the outcome of other variables. It can be calculated from the joint probability by summing over all outcomes of the other variables: `P(X) = Σy P(X, y)`. This process is called **marginalisation**.
    - **Conditional Probability `P(X|Y)`**: The probability of event X happening *given* that event Y has already happened. It's calculated as `P(X|Y) = P(X,Y) / P(Y)`.
- **Usage Scenarios/Patterns**:
    - Joint probabilities are used when we are interested in the co-occurrence of events, like the probability of seeing the character 'h' following a 't'.
    - Marginal probabilities are used when we want to know the overall probability of an event, regardless of context.
    - Conditional probabilities are fundamental for prediction and inference, such as predicting the next word in a sentence given the previous words.
- **Example**:
  In the "Scorpion" grid example, we have a 6x8 grid (48 outcomes).
  - Let X be the event "row >= 4" (south) and Y be "column >= 6" (east).
  - `P(X) = 24/48 = 0.5` (Marginal)
  - `P(Y) = 18/48 = 0.375` (Marginal)
  - `P(X,Y) = 9/48 = 0.1875` (Joint)
  - `P(X|Y) = P(X,Y) / P(Y) = (9/48) / (18/48) = 0.5` (Conditional)
- **Best Practices**:
    - Remember that `P(X|Y)` is not the same as `P(Y|X)`.
    - For independent events, `P(X,Y) = P(X)P(Y)` and `P(X|Y) = P(X)`. This is a special case and not generally true.
- **Summary**: Joint, marginal, and conditional probabilities are the building blocks for reasoning about multiple variables and their dependencies.

### Bayes' Rule
- **Motivation**: Often, we can easily measure `P(B|A)` (the probability of observing evidence B given an event A), but what we really want to know is `P(A|B)` (the probability of the event A given that we've seen the evidence B). Bayes' rule provides a way to "invert" the conditional probability.
- **Core Concepts**:
    - **Bayes' Rule**: `P(A|B) = (P(B|A) * P(A)) / P(B)`
    - **Nomenclature**:
        - `P(A|B)`: **Posterior** - what we want to know.
        - `P(B|A)`: **Likelihood** - how likely the event is to produce the evidence.
        - `P(A)`: **Prior** - our belief about the event before seeing evidence.
        - `P(B)`: **Evidence** (or Marginal Likelihood) - the total probability of observing the evidence.
- **Usage Scenarios/Patterns**:
    - Medical diagnosis: Given a positive test result (evidence), what is the probability of having a disease (event)?
    - Spam filtering: Given the words in an email (evidence), what is the probability it is spam (event)?
    - Statistical inference: Updating our belief about a hypothesis after observing data.
- **Example**: The Plague X problem.
  - `P(Plague)` (Prior) = 1/100,000
  - `P(Positive|Plague)` (Likelihood) = 0.95 (95% accuracy, 5% false negative)
  - `P(Positive|No Plague)` = 0.05 (5% false positive)
  - We want to find `P(Plague|Positive)` (Posterior).
  - Using Bayes' rule, the result is surprisingly low, around 1 in 5263.
- **Best Practices**:
    - Don't forget the denominator `P(B)`. It's crucial and is calculated by marginalising over all possibilities: `P(B) = P(B|A)P(A) + P(B|not A)P(not A)`.
    - Be aware of the "base rate fallacy": a low prior probability `P(A)` can mean the posterior `P(A|B)` is still low even with strong evidence `P(B|A)`.
- **Summary**: Bayes' rule is a fundamental tool for updating beliefs in the light of new evidence, allowing us to move from a prior to a posterior probability.

### Entropy
- **Motivation**: How can we quantify the amount of "surprise" or "uncertainty" in a probability distribution? A flat, uniform distribution is very unpredictable, while a sharply peaked one is very predictable.
- **Core Concepts**:
    - **Entropy `H(X)`**: A measure of the average uncertainty or "information content" of a random variable. For a discrete variable, it's the expected value of the log-probability.
    - **Formula**: `H(X) = -Σ P(x) * log₂(P(x))`
    - The unit is typically **bits**. One bit of entropy corresponds to the uncertainty of a fair coin flip.
- **Usage Scenarios/Patterns**:
    - In information theory, it gives the minimum number of bits needed to encode a message from a given distribution.
    - In machine learning, it's used in decision trees (information gain) and to measure the "surprise" of a model's predictions.
- **Example**: A coin toss.
  - For a fair coin (`q=0.5`), `H(X) = -0.5*log₂(0.5) - 0.5*log₂(0.5) = 1` bit. This is the maximum possible entropy for a two-outcome event, representing maximum uncertainty.
  - For a biased coin (`q=0.1`), the entropy is lower, `H(X) ≈ 0.47` bits, because the outcome is more predictable.
- **Best Practices**:
    - The base of the logarithm determines the units (base 2 for bits, base e for nats).
    - Entropy is always non-negative. `H(X) >= 0`.
- **Summary**: Entropy quantifies the uncertainty of a probability distribution, with higher values indicating more surprise and unpredictability.

## Best Practices
- **Distinguish `P(A|B)` from `P(B|A)`**: They are different and related by Bayes' rule. Confusing them is a common error.
- **Mind the Base Rate**: In Bayesian reasoning, a low prior probability can have a huge impact on the final posterior, even with seemingly strong evidence.
- **Use Log-Likelihood**: When calculating the likelihood of a long sequence of events, use the sum of log-probabilities (log-likelihood) to avoid numerical underflow from multiplying many small probabilities.
- **Entropy as a Measure of Surprise**: Use entropy to reason about the predictability of systems. A drop in entropy after a Bayesian update signifies a gain in knowledge.
