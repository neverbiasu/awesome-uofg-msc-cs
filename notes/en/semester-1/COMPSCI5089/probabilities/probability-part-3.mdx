## Summary
- **Objective**: To understand the properties of continuous random variables, learn how to model data using normal distributions, and grasp the fundamentals of statistical inference including estimation and Bayesian approaches.
- **Scope**: This note covers Probability Density Functions (PDFs), Cumulative Distribution Functions (CDFs), the Normal distribution, the Central Limit Theorem, multivariate distributions, Monte Carlo simulation, and three key methods of inference: Direct Estimation, Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE), and Bayesian Inference.

## Learning Outcomes
1. Understand the challenges and properties of continuous random variables (PDFs, CDFs).
2. Learn to model data using the Normal distribution and appreciate the significance of the Central Limit Theorem.
3. Gain insight into multivariate distributions and their parameters.
4. Understand what Monte Carlo methods are and how they can be used to approximate expectations.
5. Differentiate between population, parameters, statistics, and samples.
6. Learn how estimators work, particularly through Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE).
7. Get introduced to Bayesian inference and MCMC approaches for sampling posteriors.

## Key Concepts

### Continuous Random Variables
- **Motivation**: Unlike discrete variables which take on specific values, many real-world phenomena (e.g., height, temperature) are continuous. We need a different framework to handle probabilities for these variables.
- **Core Concepts**:
    - **Probability Density Function (PDF)**: A function `f(x)` that describes the relative likelihood for a random variable to take on a given value. The probability of the variable falling within a particular range is the integral of this function over that range.
    - **Key Property**: For any continuous variable `X`, the probability of it taking a specific value is zero, i.e., `P(X=x) = 0`. Probabilities are only defined over intervals.
    - **Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF)**: A function `F(x)` that gives the probability that the random variable `X` is less than or equal to `x`, i.e., `F(x) = P(X ≤ x)`.
    - **Support**: The set of values where the PDF is non-zero. It can be compact (a fixed interval) or infinite.

### The Normal (Gaussian) Distribution
- **Motivation**: The normal distribution is the most common continuous distribution due to its mathematical properties and its connection to the Central Limit Theorem.
- **Core Concepts**:
    - **Notation**: `X ~ N(μ, σ²)` where `μ` is the mean (location) and `σ²` is the variance (scale).
    - **PDF Formula**: The density is given by `f(x) = (1 / (σ√(2π))) * exp(-(x - μ)² / (2σ²))`. It's a symmetric bell-shaped curve centered at `μ`.
- **Central Limit Theorem (CLT)**:
    - **Concept**: The sum of a large number of independent and identically distributed random variables, regardless of their original distribution, will be approximately normally distributed.
    - **Significance**: This is why many natural processes and composite measurements tend to follow a normal distribution, making it a powerful modeling tool.

### Multivariate Distributions
- **Motivation**: Often, we need to model the relationship between multiple continuous variables simultaneously (e.g., height and weight).
- **Core Concepts**:
    - **Definition**: A distribution over a vector space `R^n`. The joint PDF must integrate to 1 over the entire space.
    - **Multivariate Normal**: Defined by a mean vector `μ` and a covariance matrix `Σ`. `μ` specifies the center of the distribution, and `Σ` describes the shape, orientation, and scale of the probability mass.
    - **Joint vs. Marginal PDF**:
        - **Joint PDF**: Density over all dimensions combined.
        - **Marginal PDF**: Density over a subset of dimensions, found by integrating out the other variables.

### Monte Carlo Methods
- **Motivation**: How can we compute expectations or other properties of distributions when the integrals are analytically intractable?
- **Core Concept**: Use random sampling to approximate the answer. To compute the expectation `E[g(X)]`, we can draw many samples `x_i` from the distribution `P(X)` and average the results of `g(x_i)`.
- **Example**: To find the expected score on a dartboard, instead of solving a complex integral of `(score_function * probability_density)`, we can simulate many dart throws according to the probability density and average the resulting scores.

### Statistical Inference
- **Motivation**: We want to infer the properties (parameters) of an entire population based on a limited set of observed samples.
- **Core Concepts**:
    - **Population**: The complete (and often infinite) set of all possible outcomes.
    - **Parameter**: A value that describes the population (e.g., the true mean `μ`).
    - **Sample**: A subset of the population that we have observed.
    - **Statistic**: A function of the sample data (e.g., the sample mean `μ̂`). It is often used as an estimator for a population parameter.

#### Direct Estimation
- **Concept**: Use specially derived functions of the data (statistics) to directly estimate population parameters.
- **Example**: The sample mean `μ̂ = (1/n) * Σx_i` is an unbiased estimator for the population mean `μ`. The sample variance `s²` is an estimator for the population variance `σ²`.

#### Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)
- **Concept**: Find the parameter values `θ` that maximize the likelihood `P(D|θ)` of observing the collected data `D`.
- **Method**: This is an optimization problem. We define the likelihood function (often the log-likelihood for convenience) and use optimization algorithms (like gradient descent) to find the parameters `θ` that maximize it.
- **Advantage**: It's a general technique that can be applied to complex models (e.g., Gaussian Mixture Models) where direct estimators are not available.

#### Bayesian Inference
- **Concept**: Update our beliefs about model parameters `θ` in light of new evidence `D`. It treats parameters themselves as random variables.
- **Bayes' Rule**: `P(θ|D) = [P(D|θ) * P(θ)] / P(D)`
    - `P(θ|D)`: **Posterior** - our updated belief about the parameters.
    - `P(D|θ)`: **Likelihood** - same as in MLE.
    - `P(θ)`: **Prior** - our initial belief about the parameters before seeing data.
    - `P(D)`: **Evidence** - a normalization constant.
- **Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)**: A class of algorithms (like Metropolis-Hastings) used to draw samples from the posterior distribution `P(θ|D)` when it's too complex to compute directly. This allows us to approximate the posterior distribution and understand the uncertainty in our parameter estimates.
</Steps>
