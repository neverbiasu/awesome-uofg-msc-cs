Computer Misuse  Act
●   Why the Computer Misuse Act came into existence  ●   What the Computer Misuse Act protects against (generally)  ●   Awareness of notable or controversial legal cases under the Computer  Misuse Act  Computer Misuse Act   -   Outline
Hacking was not  always illegal
•   Schifreen   and Gold were British computer enthusiasts who   accessed British  Telecom’s Prestel viewdata service   (an early online information system).  •   They discovered that the system’s administrator password was “1234”   —   and using it,  they gained unauthorised access to private message boxes, including one belonging to  the Duke of Edinburgh.  •   Although their intent was not malicious (more curiosity and demonstration of weak  security), they were charged under the Forgery and Counterfeiting Act (1981) because  at that time, no specific law covered hacking.  •   They were initially convicted but later   acquitted on appeal   because the law didn’t  properly apply to computer offences.  The Prestel Hack (1984 – 1987)
•   Covers individuals where the computer, the offender, or both are in the UK  •   3 offences:  •   Unauthorised access to a computer  •   Unauthorised access to a computer with intention to commit a serious crime  •   Unauthorised modification of the contents of a computer  Computer Misuse Act 1990
●   Accessing any computer, system, or data without permission.  ●   Example: logging into someone else’s account or system without consent.  There was no harm done is not a valid defence  Unauthorised access to computer material (Section 1)
●   Accessing a system without permission in order to commit another crime,  such as fraud or data theft.  ●   Example: hacking into a bank’s system to steal information or money.  Unauthorised access with intent to commit further offences  (Section 2)
●   Where the person lacks permission to modify a computer’s content but does  modifies it anyway  ○   Includes malware, web page redirection, data encryption  ○   Done with intention and Knowledge of what is being done  This is the highest level of offence in the Computer Misuse Act  Unauthorised modification of computer material (Section 3)
●   Companies want to avoid bad publicity  If you are a company and someone has modified your customers data is it  worth the bad publicity if you can quickly revert the data and prevent similar  attacks from occurring?  ●   It requires a lot of police resources to pursue these cases  ●   Penalties imposed on the guilty have been limited  Why don’t we hear about more cases?
The Case of Paul Bedworth (1993)  ●   Bedworth hacked into computers across  Europe, including the Financial  Times in London, a cancer research institute  in Brussels and European Community  offices in Luxembourg. The trial judge  observed that the full list of computers  that had been attacked would be ‘as long as a  telephone directory’.  ●   A central plank of Bedworth’s defence was the  evidence of James Griffith - Edwards, an expert  in addictive behaviour, who said that Bedworth  had a ‘nonchemical dependence’ on using a  computer. Although he knew what he was  doing was wrong, he could not prevent himself  –   much like an obsessive hand - washer   –  argued Griffith - Edwards.
The Case of Paul Bedworth (1993)  ●   There has been various  amendments and new laws  introduced to try and address the  shortcomings of existing  laws/policies  ●   The problem of technology  developing much faster than the  legal system
●   The Computer Misuse Act (1990) was created because existing UK laws  didn’t cover crimes involving computers.  ●   Three offences of the act you should know about  ●   The Computer Misuse Act is far from perfect and amendments and new  laws are proposed regularly to try and address its shortcomings  Summary
Data Protection
●   Introduce the UK’s Data Protection Act  ●   Terminologies in GDPR and UK’s Data protection Act  ●   Note: a single legislation is never sufficient   -   this is something which is  repeatedly revised  Data Protection/ GDPR   –   Outline
●   The 1st data protection act to protect individuals data against:  ○   The use of inaccurate personal information or information that is incomplete or  irrelevant  ○   The use of personal information by unauthorised persons  ○   The use of personal information for purposes other than that for which it was  collected  The 1984 Data Protection Act
●   Personal data:   relates to a living person who can be identified by the data  ○   Note: this includes expressions of opinion about the person, and intentions towards the  person  ●   Sensitive personal data : relates to ethnicity, sexual orientation, health,  political opinions, criminal record, etc  Key terminology
●   Data processing:   obtaining, recording, holding the data, or carrying out any  operations on it (extensive definition of ‘operations’)  ●   It has a very wide definition  Key terminology
●   Data subject:   the person who is the subject of the personal data  ●   Data controller:   determines how or why personal data is processed  ●   Data processor:   processes personal data on behalf of the data controller  Note: the data processor need not be employed by the data controller but they  will need to be in communication with each other in some capacity  Key terminology
●   The 1st data was introduced in 1984  ○   The 1984 act was later superseded by the 1998  ○   The 1998 act was then superseded by the 2018 act  ●   Functionally they aim to serve individuals in the same way  The various UK Data Protection Acts
●   The protections it gives individuals  ●   In this course, we will refer to/ use a condensed summary of the 1998 act  ●   These protections are also present in the in 2018 act  If you want to read the latest version:   https://www.gov.uk/data - protection  What do you need to know about the Data Protection Act?
Individuals should know how their data is being used.
Purpose limitation
Only collect the   minimum amount of data necessary   for the stated purpose.
Inaccurate data should be corrected or deleted.
Data should be   kept only as long as necessary   for the purpose it was collected.

Personal data must be   processed securely , protecting it from unauthorised or unlawful access, loss, or damage.  Organisations are   responsible for demonstrating compliance   —   they must have policies and records to prove it.

●   Data subjects have the right to receive:  ○   A description of the personal data being held about them  ○   An explanation of the purpose for which it is being held and processed  ○   A description of the people or organisations to which it may be disclosed  ○   An intelligible statement of the specific data held about them  ○   A description of the source of the data  The rights of data subjects
●   Data subjects have the right to receive:  ○   Prevent processing likely to cause damage and distress  ○   Prevent processing for the purposes of direct marketing  ○   Claim compensation in the case of damage caused by illegal processing of  personal data  The rights of data subjects

Why introduce GDPR?
●   GDPR requires a “lawful basis'” for processing personal data,  typically through seeking consent, or determining “legitimate interest”  in processing.  Consider the example of an AR headset: A device equipped with sensors capable of  capturing extensive amounts of the user’s surrounding reality  Many of the “risky” activities are not ruled out by GDPR but rather would have to be  legally justified through e.g. garnering user consent  ●   E.g.   the capture and manipulation of a bystander’s image  But GDPR is not perfect!
●   An AR device can provide real - time navigational information to a blind user,  e.g. tell them how far away someone is, if they are walking towards them,  what they look like, what they are wearing, their expression, who they are (if  recognised by the device), etc  Access to information : What we find is that people are  comfortable with some users having access  to more information than others:  ●   Tensions regarding who should have  access to what information  ●   Does the law need to be reflective of  this as well?
